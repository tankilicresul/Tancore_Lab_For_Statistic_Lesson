How to Build a Good Model?
Ease of understanding the model:
use mnemonic (related) names for variables and constraints
Ease of detecting errors in the model:
avoid formulation errors
unbounded or infeasible models are usually indicators of error
examine if a known solution is feasible
examine if the optimal solution makes sense
include sensible bounds on decision variables

How to Build a Good Model?
Ease of computing the solution:
realistic assumptions may simplify the model
some procedures may detect redundancies and fix some variables to reduce the problem size
some models have a special structure, which can be exploited to solve these models easily, e.g., assignment problem, transportation problem
some models can be decomposed into smaller models

Units of measurement:
make sure that left-hand-side and right-hand-side of the constraints have the same units
disparity in the sizes of coefficients leads to unstable models

	Supply Chain Management is the integration of key business processes from end user through original suppliers, that provides products, services, and information that add value for customers and other stakeholders.

Full Truckload Distribution
A company operates several warehouses that supply products to a set of geographically dispersed customers. 
Each warehouse has a limited amount of inventory available for shipment. 
Deliveries are made using full-truckload transportation, where each truck has a fixed carrying capacity and incurs a lane-specific cost depending on the warehouse–customer pair. 
Multiple trucks can be dispatched on the same lane if needed. 
The company must decide how many trucks to send from each warehouse to each customer so that customer demands are satisfied and warehouse capacity limits are respected, while minimizing total transportation cost.

Simulation models
Validation is the process of checking whether a simulation model accurately represents the real-world system it is meant to imitate.
A model is valid if its outputs behave like the real system’s outputs within an acceptable level of accuracy.
Validation answers the question: Does my model capture reality well enough to be useful?
INDR 100
6
--- Slide 7 ---
Validation methods
1. Compare model output with real-world data
	- Check whether averages, variances, distributions, or performance measures match observed historical data.
2. Face validity
	- Ask domain experts (e.g., managers, operators) if the model’s logic and behavior look reasonable and realistic.
3. Sensitivity analysis
	- Change input parameters and see if the model behaves in a believable and consistent way.
	- Test the model with very high or very low input values to see if it reacts logically.
4. Trace / step-through debugging
	- Follow individual entities (customers, jobs, vehicles) through the simulation to check if the logic works as intended.
5. Comparison with simpler models
	- Compare results with theoretical formulas (e.g., M/M/1 queue results), or
simpler hand-made calculations.
	- Compare with the results with a simpler but validated simulation

Course Description 
Introduction to industrial engineering concepts. Fundamentals of systems analysis and modeling. Basics 
of production and service systems.  
   
Course Objectives 
The objective of this course is to introduce the profession of industrial engineering and our academic 
curriculum to freshmen students. Fundamentals of systems analysis and modeling and the basics of 
production and service systems will be presented. Several industrial engineering topics with their 
applications in real life systems, modeling of real-life problems and the solution techniques of these 
models will be introduced.  
   
Learning Outcomes 
The students are expected to understand the profession of industrial engineering and its relation with the 
production and service systems. The students will acquire the fundamentals of systems thinking, analysis 
and modeling of real-life systems and the basic solution techniques to these models.  
  
Teaching Methods 
The objective of this course is to introduce the profession of industrial engineering and our academic 
curriculum to freshmen students. The course aims to fulfill this task by presenting a compendium of 
current topics and applications of industrial engineering via lectures and videos. Course material 
including syllabus and lecture notes, will be at LearnHub.  
 
Textbook 
• Turner, W.C., Mize J.H, Case K.E, Nazemetz J.W., Introduction to Industrial and Systems 
Engineering, 3rd ed., Prentice Hall. (recommended, optional) 
 
Course topics (tentative) 
• Introduction to Industrial Engineering (2 weeks) 
• Overview of areas in Industrial Engineering (1-3 weeks each) 
o Production and Service Systems 
o Modeling Concepts and Introduction to Linear Programming 
o Introduction to Logistics and Supply Chain Management 
o Network Models and Transportation Problems 
o Probability

Production improvement does not necessarily 
mean productivity improvement.
(Productivity ≠beingproductive)
qSuppose a bank processed 1,000 checks yesterday, using

unchanged, because 1,000/20 is equal to 50 checks per

Efficiency and Effectiveness 
for productivity improvement
qEfficiency is a necessary but not a satisfactory condition for 
productivity. In fact, both effectiveness and efficiency are necessary 
in order to be productive.
qEfficiency is the ratio of actual output generated to the expected 
(or standard) output prescribed. 
qEffectiveness, on the other hand, is the degree to which the 
relevant goals or objectives are achieved.
qEffectiveness involves first determining the relevant (right) goals or 
objectives and then achieving them. 
–If, for example, nine out of ten relevant goals are achieved, the 
effectiveness is 90%. One can be very efficient and still not be 
productive. 
13

productivity improvement
qFor example, suppose a dentist removes a 
patient’s tooth in half the usual time and boasts 
(show off) his assistants:
–“I have been twice as efficient as in the past.” 
qThe assistants, who view the situation say 
something differently: 
–“What a disaster – the dentist removed the wrong tooth!” 
qHere, the dentist’s effectiveness was zero 
because he did not achieve the relevant goal, 
even though his efficiency improved by 200%.

Mathematical Programming Models
❑ Relate decision variables (controllable inputs) 
with fixed or variable parameters (uncontrollable 
inputs)
❑ Maximize or minimize some criteria (objective 
function) subject to limits (constraints)
▪ Objectives (minimize cost, maximize profit, etc.)
▪ Constraints (capacities, budget limits, etc.)

• Filsiz Boya company owns a small paint factory that produces 
both interior and exterior house paints for wholesale 
distribution. 
• Two basic raw materials, R and S, are used to manufacture the 
paints.  
• One ton of exterior paint requires 1 ton of raw material R and 3 
tons of material S, whereas 1 ton of interior paint requires 2 tons 
of raw material R and 1 ton of raw material S. 
• The wholesale price per ton is 3000 TL for exterior paint and 
2000 TL for interior paint.
• The total amount of raw material R (S) is 6 (11).
• Also, we know that the maximum demand for exterior is 3 tons, 
whereas the demand for exterior cannot exceed that of interior 
by more than 1 ton.
